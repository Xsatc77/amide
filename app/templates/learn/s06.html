A Certificate of Analysis (COA) is a lab report on a sample of a product. It's the main tool you have for checking a vendor's claims. But a COA is only as good as who made it and what it tested.

Who made it

TypeWhat it isHow much to trust it
In-house or vendor-madeThe vendor tested it themselves, or just made a documentVery little
Third-party, ordered by the vendorAn outside lab, but the vendor picks what to send and what to showSome, but it can be cherry-picked
Independent third-partySomeone other than the vendor bought a vial and sent it to a labThe most trust. This is the one that tells you something
Community testingA group pools money to buy and test productsOften strong, especially when repeated

What it tested

  • HPLC (purity). Shows how much of what's detected is the main compound versus other things. It does not by itself prove the main compound is the right one.
  • Mass spectrometry (identity). Measures the molecular weight, which confirms the compound is what it claims to be.
  • Quantity (net peptide content). How much real peptide is in the vial. High purity does not mean the vial holds the milligrams on the label.
  • Endotoxin and sterility. Matter most for anything you'll inject. Many vendors never test these.

Batch-specific or generic? A COA must match the lot number on your vial. A report for a different lot, or no lot at all, tells you nothing about what you hold.

The rule of thumb: a good report is independent, batch-specific, covers identity, purity and quantity, and can be verified with the lab.
