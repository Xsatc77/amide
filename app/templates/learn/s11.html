Most reactions are mild. A few are not. Know the difference before you need it.

Call Emergency Services Immediately (don't wait to see if it passes) For

  • Hives or a rash spreading across the body.
  • Swelling of the lips, tongue, face or throat.
  • Wheezing, tightness in the chest, or trouble breathing.
  • Feeling faint, a racing heart, or confusion soon after an injection.
  • If you have a prescribed adrenaline (epinephrine) injector, use it as instructed, and still call for help.

See A Clinician The Same Day For

  • Redness that spreads, a hot or very painful spot, pus, or a lump that grows.
  • Fever or chills after an injection.
  • Severe or persistent nausea or vomiting, severe headache, or dizziness that doesn't settle.
  • Anything that simply feels wrong, especially if it's new.

Mild and usually fine to watch: a small red spot, slight tenderness, an occasional bruise. Note it, and don't inject into that spot again until it has cleared.

What To Do In Any Reaction

  1. Stop. Don't take another dose until you've sorted out what happened.
  2. Keep the evidence. Don't throw the vial away. Keep the vial, packaging, the lot number, and the syringe if it's safe to do so. Take photos.
  3. Write it down. Time of the dose, the compound, the amount, the lot, and what happened and when. Amide's dose and journal records already hold most of this.
  4. Tell the clinician everything, including that the product came from the gray market, and show them the lot number. Being honest here is what lets them treat you properly, and good clinicians would rather know.
  5. Report it if your country has a reporting scheme. In the United States, that's the FDA's MedWatch program, and other countries' medicines regulators have equivalents. Reports from people who used unapproved products help regulators spot bad batches.

If someone else is reacting: stay with them, call for help, and tell the responders what was injected. Don't leave them alone.
