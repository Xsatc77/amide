You don't need a chemistry degree, just a checklist. Go through a report in this order.

1. The Header

  • Lab name and contact details. Can you reach the lab and ask whether the report is real?
  • Date, and the name of the sample.
  • Lot / batch number. Does it match your vial?

2. The Purity Chart (HPLC)

  • You'll see a line with peaks. You want one tall main peak and little else.
  • The report gives a percentage, for example "98%." That's the main peak's share of everything detected.
  • Lots of small peaks mean impurities. A chart cut off at the edges, or only a number with no chart, is a warning.

3. The Identity Chart (Mass Spectrometry)

  • The report gives an observed mass and an expected mass for that compound.
  • They should match closely. If they don't, it may not be what it says.
  • Don't worry if you see several peaks for one compound. That's normal, because it can appear carrying different charges.

4. Quantity, Endotoxin And Sterility

  • Is there a number for how much peptide is really in the vial?
  • Is there an endotoxin result? If you inject, you want one.

Consistency In Mass Is Fundamental

Don't only ask whether one report looks good. Ask whether the reports agree with each other. Collect the quantity results from different lots and compare them to what the vendor promises.

  • Say the label promises 10 mg. One lot comes back 150% high, the next is overfilled by 70%, the one after is underfilled by 10%. (Made-up numbers, but the pattern is real.)
  • If the bottles aren't consistent, how can you dose that peptide properly, down to the microgram, when what's in the vial swings that much from one to the next?
  • A 150% overfill isn't extra product. It's a red flag that there are quality-control problems in the factory. A process that can overfill by that much can underfill just as easily.
  • A vendor you can trust shows results that land close to the label, lot after lot. Tight and boring beats generous and random.

Red Flags

  • No lot number, or a different lot.
  • A report with right-looking numbers but no chart.
  • Fonts, logos or layouts that look edited or pasted in.
  • The same report used for different products.
  • A lab that doesn't exist, or won't answer.
  • Everything perfect, always. Real batches vary a little.
  • Amounts that swing widely from lot to lot, whether over or under the label: an overfill is a quality problem, not a bonus.

What a COA can't tell you: it describes one tested sample. It can't promise that your vial matches it. That's why repeated, independent testing counts for more than any single document.
