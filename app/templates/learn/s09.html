Yes, that's the honest question, and plenty of people ask it. Here's what's involved.

It's smaller than you think

These aren't the needles from the doctor's office. Insulin-type syringes use a very thin, short needle meant to go just under the skin. Many people say they feel little more than a pinch, and some feel nothing.

How often depends on the compound

Some are used daily, some more than once a day, some weekly. That schedule comes from a clinician or from the evidence on that specific compound, not from this tutorial. Treat any schedule you read in a chat group as unverified.

Reading a syringe: units are not milligrams

The marks on an insulin syringe measure volume. Play with the two vials below: the same number of units holds a different amount of peptide depending on how much water the vial was mixed with.

Keeping it clean

  • A new, sterile syringe every time. Never reuse one, and never share.
  • Wash your hands. Wipe the vial stopper and your skin with alcohol and let it dry.
  • Don't touch the needle to anything.
  • Rotate your sites so you don't keep injecting the same spot. Amide keeps track of your last site and suggests the next one.
  • Put used needles in a proper sharps container, not the bin.

If you hate needles

  • Look away, breathe out slowly, and relax the spot.
  • Use the finest, shortest needle suited to the job, and let the alcohol dry completely, since wet skin stings.
  • A cold pack held there for a moment first can help.
  • Rehearse the motions with an empty syringe so you know the steps before it counts.
  • Some compounds exist in forms that aren't injected. It's reasonable to ask whether that's an option.

Keep records. Which vial, which lot, what date, which site, and how you felt. Amide is built to keep them for you, and section 10 explains why they matter.
